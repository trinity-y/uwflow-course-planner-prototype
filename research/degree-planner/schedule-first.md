# A degree schedule you can follow or change

**Give me a course schedule I could follow through graduation. Let me make as many of the decisions as I want, and show me what happens when I change my mind.** The term I’m choosing for deserves the most attention. The rest of the schedule gives that decision context.

## What the student owns

- **A schedule** holds terms, available load, and choices. A choice can be a course in a term, a course that still needs a term, or a place reserved for a kind of course. A favourite or a shortlist can help fill a choice without committing the student to it.
- **A record** describes what actually happened. Every schedule can use the same attempts, outcomes, awarded credits, and individual rulings. Correcting a historical record is a deliberate action. Trying a different future leaves that record intact.
- **A set of requirements** comes from the credentials and calendar editions the student selects for this schedule. The schedule exists on its own. Applying a different combination lets the student see which existing choices still count and which gaps open up. When coverage is incomplete, they can still arrange courses and inspect the checks Flow can make.

The key distinction within a schedule is **what the student has decided and what Flow has proposed to fill the rest**. Both can appear in a complete, named route. If a student settles on a proposed course, it becomes their choice. If they care only about taking a science elective that winter, that is the decision Flow should preserve when it suggests another route.

## What happens when you open it

1. **Bring what you have.** Import a transcript, reuse an existing Flow history, type courses, or open a blank schedule. Confirm the inferred program and applicable calendar. Ask about an uncertain credit or term only when it would change the answer the student needs.
2. **Get a plan at the level you want.** Someone who wants a starting point can press **Autocomplete my degree** and receive a saved, editable schedule with named courses through their target finish when Flow finds a route. Someone arriving with their own plan can work entirely by hand. Both can see what depends on an unconfirmed offering, credit, or approval.
3. **Work where the decision is.** Open the current or next study term for course selection, the full schedule for a co-op or exchange change, or a desired course to plan backwards. Changing the view keeps the same schedule, planning horizon and commitments. The full grid and course collections are useful ways into the work without becoming required steps.
4. **Choose with the evidence in reach.** From a requirement, see counted courses, other eligible courses and outstanding conditions. From a course, see where it counts, where it could count, prerequisites already met or missing, and future courses the student cares about. Put term availability and optional Flow reviews beside those differences. A single named example can fill a future elective while its alternatives remain visible.
5. **Try a change before adopting it.** For “drop this course this term,” hold that decision fixed, retain everything else possible, and show the smallest workable repair found. Say whether the target finish and a particular goal course survive, and what has to move. Accepting the change revises the schedule. Actual enrolment and academic history change when the student records or imports what happened.

A complete route should be available to someone willing to follow it. Some choices on that route may be freely swappable, others may need to happen by a particular term, and still others may depend on a ruling. Showing which is which makes a full draft useful to students who want to keep their options open.

## How to read progress

Every schedule has a progress view. For the combined degree and each credential, show **earned on the record**, **covered if the scheduled courses pass**, and **still open or awaiting confirmation**. Use units for unit requirements and named conditions for prerequisites and milestones. Historical attempts remain visible even when they contribute no credit. An approved substitution can settle one requirement while leaving a total-unit requirement open.

A temporary preview suits a single swap. A named duplicate suits a durable question such as “with exchange” or “with a minor.” History serves a third purpose, recovering what the student had before an edit. Two live alternatives can share corrections to actual history and keep their own future choices and rules.
