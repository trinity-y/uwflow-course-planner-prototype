# The core pitch

Open UW Flow to the next term you’re choosing courses for. Put in what you already know. Flow shows how those choices fit your degree and what they make possible later. You can finish with a list for one term or keep planning until you can see a route through graduation.

## Three things and how they relate

| Concept | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Program** | What I intend to finish. My chosen degree, major and minor, with their applicable calendar years, supply the requirements. Flow considers them together so the use of a course across credentials is visible. |
| **Record** | What has happened or been decided about me. This includes course attempts and outcomes, awarded credits, and the scope of an individual decision. A pending ruling stays identifiable as a question. |
| **Plan** | What I intend to do. It contains choices I can place in terms. I can name the course, name the kind of course I need, or leave the term open. I can keep a work term free or set a lighter load where that matters. |

The ordinary thing a student edits is **a choice in their plan**. “Statistics next winter,” “a statistics course sometime,” and “this particular course, whenever it fits” are all useful entries. Selecting a course for an open choice makes the same entry more specific. Moving it changes the timing. A personal-interest course belongs in the plan even if it fills only a free elective.

The requirements view comes from reading the program against the record, then against the record plus the plan. The useful piece in between is an **explanation for each requirement**. What does it ask for? What counted? What would a planned choice contribute? What is still missing or uncertain? Where did that conclusion come from? That is the information the interface needs from a checker. Each faculty can express its own rules behind those answers.

## What the student actually sees

The main workspace is **Next term**. It contains the student’s course choices. Alongside it is **Requirements**, grouped in familiar calendar language such as degree, major, and minor. Later terms are available when a decision reaches that far.

For a new student with a chosen program, offer the department’s starting sequence where one is known. Leave its elective choices open. A student bringing a list can enter it directly. Someone arriving from a course page can add that one course and see its known prerequisites immediately. Identifying the program adds degree fit. Adding past coursework makes the answer personal. Each entry point leads back to the same term.

Imagine a student choosing their last winter course. In this fictional example, their program requires one methods course and there are two eligible choices.

| What the student does | What the requirements view says |
| --- | --- |
| Opens **Methods · choose one** | “Still open on the record you entered.” Expanding the row shows the rule, qualifying choices, and any missing information. |
| Reserves winter for Methods | “Place reserved in winter. Course still to choose.” The term has space set aside and earned progress stays the same. |
| Compares Research Methods and Field Methods | Either would meet Methods if passed. Research Methods is also a prerequisite for a fall studio the student wants. That consequence gives them a reason to choose it now. |
| Picks Research Methods | The row reads “Planned for winter.” From the course they can see both what it satisfies and what it opens. From the requirement they can jump back to the course in winter. |
| Returns after winter | Recording a pass updates earned progress. A withdrawal keeps the attempt in their history and reopens the choice. Only conclusions that depend on the result need attention. |

A student with exchange credit uses the same view. Suppose 0.5 elective units have been awarded and a request to use them for Methods is pending. The units appear on the record. The Methods row shows the open question. The student can see the effect of taking a winter methods course or leaving room pending the decision. If they later record approval, the row shows the substitution and its origin. Neither choice requires a separate exchange planner.

## Why it gets progressively more useful

Knowing more about a student and asking for more automation are independent. A student with a complete record can pick every course manually and get precise feedback. A newcomer with only a program can ask for a starter term and see the assumptions behind it. Both can edit any choice. An unresolved requirement affects the conclusions that depend on it and leaves the rest of the plan useful.

The first version I would test has one manual action, **add or move a choice**, and one assisted action, **show me courses that fit this choice**. A published sequence simply starts the same plan with some choices already filled. A future whole-plan suggestion would propose more of the same edits. The student can take as much help as they want and still own every decision.
