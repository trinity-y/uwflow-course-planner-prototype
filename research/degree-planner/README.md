# UW Flow course planner · Requirements and research index

**Help a Waterloo student build a term-by-term degree schedule they can follow, and make a decision this term with confidence about what comes after it.** A student can start with a full suggested route, their own schedule, or a single course. How much they plan or delegate is up to them.

The [schedule-first proposal](schedule-first.md) and [review of the branch prototype](prototype-review.md) incorporate the team’s whole-degree autocomplete requirement. The [earlier term-first pitch](experience.md) preserves the alternative starting point for comparison. The requirements below describe what students should be able to do.

## What the product needs to support

| A student needs to be able to… | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| **Start with what they know.** Bring one course, a chosen program, an existing spreadsheet, or part of a record. | A new student may need a starting path. Asking an upper-year student to reconstruct six terms before answering one question makes their spreadsheet the better tool. [Student planning habits](research.md#what-people-are-already-doing) |
| **Start from a transcript and ask for an entire degree schedule in one action.** Receive a concrete, editable route to the target finish for supported credentials when Flow finds one. | A student who has no course preferences should be able to follow the suggested route. Identify uncertain inputs and unconfirmed offerings. When a complete route cannot be found, keep the partial result and identify the unresolved decisions. [Schedule-first proposal](schedule-first.md) |
| **Own a schedule independently of its rules.** Build or duplicate it, then apply a credential combination or another calendar to see the effect. | Students with unsupported rules still need a manual planning surface. A student adding a minor mid-degree should be able to compare what changes while keeping their existing schedule and past record. [Prototype review](prototype-review.md) |
| **Choose both what to take and when to take it.** Name a course without settling its term, or reserve room before choosing a course. | Some students work backwards from a favourite course. Others need to know how many choices they can leave open. A useful plan can be precise about next term and rough about next year. [Student perspectives](research.md#some-students-want-a-course-before-they-want-a-plan) |
| **See and explain progress.** Understand what is earned, what planned courses could cover, what remains, and what Flow lacks evidence to assess. | The survey summary emphasizes visualization and progress. Students also ask where a course counts and how many units they still need. One course can satisfy two permitted conditions while earning its units once. [Course-counting and elective-room examples](testing-and-release.md#three-exercises-i-ran) |
| **See the consequence of a choice, including during a term.** Try dropping or switching an enrolled course and compare the remaining routes while that change stays in force. | Losing a route to a favourite elective and delaying graduation are different outcomes. A useful comparison shows prerequisites, downstream options, requirement contributions, offering evidence and relevant student experience. It also distinguishes what can wait from a decision due this term. [Prototype comparison](prototype-review.md#how-to-make-choices-without-a-giant-decision-tree) |
| **Choose how much help to accept.** Work entirely by hand, get help with one opening, or use a starting sequence. Keep personal priorities when circumstances change. | A student may know three courses are fixed and want advice only on the fourth. An interesting elective, a reduced load, or a free work term can matter more to them than finishing early. Community reviews can inform that judgement. [Protected choices](research.md#the-student-may-know-exactly-which-part-to-protect) · [Student judgement](research.md#people-want-student-judgement-in-context) |
| **Plan across faculties and combinations of credentials.** Know what Flow can check for their degree, minor, and applicable calendar. | Waterloo examples include different calendar years within a double major, fractional units, separate labs, and restrictions on sharing credit. The campus-wide aim is meaningful core progress for every in-scope student. Coverage needs to be visible when it is partial. [Six faculty examples](research.md#six-waterloo-examples-that-should-shape-the-design) |
| **Represent what actually happened and keep planning around an unknown.** This includes repeated courses, exchange or Laurier credits, leave, and individual approvals. | An external course might earn units while its use for a named requirement is pending. A course-entry waiver and a degree substitution answer different questions. The student should still get answers everywhere the unresolved issue has no bearing. [Irregular academic records](data-and-coverage.md#irregular-records-are-ordinary-planning-cases) |
| **Return and revise without losing earlier decisions.** See which source or personal fact explains a changed answer. | Students return after grades, offering changes, and advising decisions. New maintainers also need to distinguish a new calendar, a correction to an old rule, and a decision that applies to one person. [Long-term maintenance](data-and-coverage.md#keeping-it-credible-after-the-original-team-leaves) |

## Research index

- [Student experiences, all six faculties, existing planners, and course-choice studies](research.md). Use this to challenge assumptions about whom the product serves.
- [Sources, coverage, exceptions, and ownership](data-and-coverage.md). Use this to decide what Flow can responsibly tell a particular student.
- [Worked cases and proposed student and advisor tests](testing-and-release.md). These small model exercises and the earlier term-first rollout are a basis for comparison. The schedule-first proposal treats full-degree autocomplete as a core flow. Usability and adoption still need direct testing.

**The simplest acceptance test** is to give a student a partly filled term and a course they want next year. Can they choose what to take this term, explain what stays possible, and leave the rest undecided?
